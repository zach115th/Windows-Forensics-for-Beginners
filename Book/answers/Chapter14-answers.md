# Chapter 14 — Guide to the Final Lab

*Companion notes for [`Chapter14.md`](../Chapter14.md).*

**What this file is, and why it is different from the others.** Chapters 1 through 13 each close with a Review Questions section and a Practical Exercises section, and each has a matching `ChapterNN-answers.md` file in this folder. Chapter 14 has neither. It is the book's closing chapter, and it is reflective rather than technical. §14.1 is a competency inventory. §14.2 and §14.3 map the appendices and the deliberately out-of-scope territory. §14.4 proposes a practice cadence, and §14.5 names the habits that make findings defensible. §14.6, *A Final Lab: Plan Your Learning Roadmap*, is itself a five-step structured exercise. There are no questions to answer and no exercises to work in the usual sense, and there is no `Labs/Lab14/` folder.

This file is therefore not an answer key. It is a guide to working §14.6 well, because that section is easy to complete and hard to complete honestly. A roadmap filled in badly is worse than no roadmap: it produces the feeling of having planned without any of the effects. What follows covers what a good self-assessment looks like and how to choose focus areas by gap rather than by interest. It also covers what makes a practice routine survive contact with an operational schedule, and the failure modes that make most learning plans quietly die in week three.

## Working §14.6

### Step 1 — Self-Assessment

**What you should end up with:** The 13-row table from §14.6 filled in, every row rated 1 to 5, with a note on each row that says *why* the number is what it is.

**How to get there:** Rate against demonstrated capability, not familiarity. The question is not "do I know what a shellbag is?" It is "could I, without looking anything up, parse `UsrClass.dat`, interpret the output, and state what the entries do and do not establish about user activity?" A useful forcing device for each row: name the last time you actually did the thing, on real or lab data, unassisted. If you cannot name an instance, the honest rating is a 2 regardless of how much you have read about it. A second device that works well for the analytical chapters: for each row, state the specific investigative question that artifact family answers, and then state its limits. If you can do the first but not the second, you are at 3, not 4. Knowing what an artifact proves is the easy half. Knowing what it does not prove is what the findings in Chapter 13 rest on.

**What good looks like:** The notes column is filled in, and it is more useful than the numbers. "3 — can parse and read MFT output, have not recovered a deleted file from unallocated in over a year" is actionable. A bare 3 is not. The chapter's own instruction is the standard: *a 3 on memory is fine. A 3 you wrote down is more useful than a 4 you talked yourself into.* Expect the distribution to be lumpy rather than uniform, because real exposure is lumpy. Most responders are strong on whatever their environment throws at them weekly and weak everywhere else. A table where every row is a 3 or 4 has almost certainly been rated on familiarity rather than capability. Go back and apply the "when did I last do this unassisted" test to every row that feels comfortable. Date the assessment. It is the baseline you re-run in six months, and an undated one cannot show movement.

### Step 2 — Pick Two or Three Focus Areas

**What you should end up with:** Two focus areas, or three at the absolute maximum, each with the chapter or topic named, a written statement of why it matters for your role, and a target proficiency date.

**How to get there:** Take the lowest scores from Step 1 and filter them through role relevance. That is what the "why this matters for my role" line is for, and it is not a formality. A 2 on memory forensics is a serious gap for an IR consultant who arrives at live hosts. It is a much smaller one for an analyst whose organization's hosts are all VMs with mandatory snapshot-on-alert. A 2 on network forensics matters more if you have no EDR than if you have full telemetry. The right selection is the intersection of "I scored low" and "this comes up in the work I actually do." Where those two sets do not overlap at all, pick the low score anyway and say why in the notes. The lowest scores tend to be the areas you have been avoiding.

**What good looks like:** The count is two or three, not five. The discipline of the cap is the whole point of the step. The "why this matters for my role" line names a case you could not work, or worked badly, rather than saying a topic is important in general. "I could not answer whether the process was injected when it mattered on the October case" beats "memory forensics is a core skill." The target date is real: three to six months out, on the calendar, and specific enough to be missed. The definition of "proficient" is written down alongside it, because a target date without a definition of the target cannot be met or missed. "Can run a full Volatility 3 triage on an unfamiliar image and produce a written finding without a reference" is a target. "Get better at memory" is not.

### Step 3 — Identify One Resource per Focus Area

**What you should end up with:** Exactly one named resource per focus area, with an estimated total hour commitment and a cadence.

**How to get there:** One resource, chosen and named, is the instruction, and it is the part people ignore. The §14.4.1 list is the starting point. It offers [The DFIR Report](https://thedfirreport.com/) for artifact-level intrusion case studies, and [CyberDefenders](https://cyberdefenders.org/) and [Blue Team Labs Online](https://blueteamlabs.online/) for hands-on challenges, including memory and disk scenarios. It also offers [13Cubed](https://www.youtube.com/@13Cubed) for focused Windows artifact walkthroughs, [Eric Zimmerman's training material](https://ericzimmerman.github.io/) for the tools this book leans on, [EVTX-to-MITRE-Attack](https://github.com/mdecrevoisier/EVTX-to-MITRE-Attack) for testing event-log hunt-pack filters against real attacker samples rather than synthetic data, and [MITRE ATT&CK](https://attack.mitre.org/) as a technique catalogue to trace through the artifacts in this book. Estimate the hours honestly, then compare that number against your cadence. If the resource is 40 hours and the cadence is one hour a week, the target date in Step 2 is wrong. One of the two has to change now rather than in month three.

**What good looks like:** The resource is specific enough to open tomorrow morning — a named challenge, a named image, a named playlist, not "some CTFs." The hour estimate and the cadence multiply out to less than the interval to the target date, with slack. You checked that arithmetic rather than assuming it. A focus area with no obvious resource is itself a finding. It usually means the focus area is defined too broadly to be practiced, such as "get better at Windows internals," and needs narrowing to something a resource could plausibly cover. Prefer resources that produce an *artifact* — a completed challenge writeup, a parsed image with your notes, a rule you wrote — over resources you only consume. Six months later, consumed material is indistinguishable from unread material, and produced material is not.

### Step 4 — Schedule the Practice

**What you should end up with:** Recurring calendar blocks, actually created, at the cadence from Step 3.

**How to get there:** Open the calendar and create them now, in the session where you are filling in the roadmap. This step determines whether any of the preceding steps have an effect. It is a two-minute action that people almost always defer to a "later" that does not arrive. The §14.4 cadence is a reasonable default for an operational responder: one hour a week of focused practice on a chosen artifact source, one half-day a quarter for deeper work such as a full investigation of a public disk or memory image, and one internal share a quarter.

**What good looks like:** The blocks exist in the calendar system your organization actually uses, not in a note. They sit where operational work is least likely to eat them, such as early morning or a fixed slot the team knows about. The end of the day is the first thing an incident consumes. There is a written rule for conflicts, and the chapter states the right one: if a block conflicts with operational work, reschedule it *the same week* rather than letting it lapse. That rule separates a routine that survives from one that quietly stops. The failure is never a decision to abandon the plan. It is three consecutive weeks of reasonable-sounding deferrals. The cadence matters more than the volume. A consistent hour a week beats a heroic eight-hour session every two months, because the eight-hour session is the one that gets cancelled and never rebooked. The half-day quarterly block is booked for the whole year at once, not quarter by quarter. A quarterly commitment that has to be re-made every quarter is really four separate decisions, and at least two of them will go the wrong way.

### Step 5 — Plan One Sharing Activity

**What you should end up with:** One teaching or sharing event with a named format, a named audience, a topic, and a date inside the next 90 days.

**How to get there:** Pick a format your organization already has a slot for, such as a brown-bag, a wiki page, a walkthrough at a team meeting, or a mentoring session with a junior analyst. Inventing a new forum is a second project, and it will not happen. Then pick the topic deliberately. Two choices work. The chapter you are most confident in produces a good session and consolidates what you already have. The area you are reinforcing produces a harder session and consolidates far more. The second is better if you can tolerate it, because preparing to teach something you are shaky on is the fastest way to find the gaps in it.

**What good looks like:** The date is committed externally, with an invite sent, a slot booked, and someone else expecting it. A private commitment to teach in 90 days has no mechanism to make it happen. The audience is real and specific. The topic passes the chapter's own test: *if you cannot explain a registry persistence pattern to a less-experienced colleague, you do not yet understand it well enough to defend a finding based on it.* That test is the honest reason this step is in the roadmap and not an optional extra. Preparing material exposes the places where your understanding is a memorized conclusion rather than a chain of reasoning. It exposes them in a room where being corrected is cheap, rather than in a report review where it is not.

## What a completed roadmap should look like

A finished §14.6 output is short — one page, maybe two — and it has five properties. It is *dated*, so you can compare the next assessment against it. It is *specific*, naming chapters, resources, dates, and formats rather than intentions. It is *narrow*, with two or three focus areas rather than a list of everything you scored below a 4 on. It is *scheduled*, with the calendar blocks already created rather than planned. And it is *externally committed* in at least one place, the sharing activity, so that at least one element of it has a mechanism outside your own motivation.

Re-run Step 1 against the same table in six months without looking at the original ratings first, then compare. The comparison is more informative than either assessment alone. The rows worth examining are the focus areas that did not move. Usually the cause is a resource that was consumed rather than practiced, or a cadence that lapsed in week three and was never restarted.

## Common failure modes on the final lab

- **Rating yourself on familiarity rather than demonstrated capability.** This failure invalidates everything downstream, because you select the focus areas from these numbers. Having read the chapter, watched a walkthrough, and understood the concept produces a comfortable feeling that is very hard to distinguish from competence. The two diverge sharply the first time you have to produce a finding under time pressure without a reference open. The test that separates them is behavioral: name the last time you did this unassisted, on real or lab data, and produced an output someone else read. No instance, no rating above a 2. Applied honestly, this test drags several rows down by a full point. Those newly lowered rows are usually the ones that most needed attention.

- **Picking focus areas by interest rather than by gap.** Memory forensics and malware analysis are the two topics people choose because they are the most interesting. They are frequently not the topics that would most improve your casework. The self-assessment exists to override this instinct, and it only works if you take the lowest scores rather than the most appealing ones. The "why this matters for my role" line in the Step 2 template is the check. If you cannot write a role-specific reason that references work you actually do, you have chosen by interest. That is not fatal, and enthusiasm sustains practice in a way that duty does not. But make the substitution consciously, and write down what you deprioritized to make room, so that the gap you skipped is on the record rather than quietly forgotten.

- **Scheduling practice with no calendar commitment.** "One hour a week" written in a document is an aspiration. "One hour a week" as a recurring calendar block with a same-week reschedule rule is a routine. The difference is mechanism, not motivation. Nothing in an operational week creates space for practice by default. You have to take the space in advance and defend it, and the defense is the reschedule rule. An incident *will* eat the block, and the only question is whether it comes back. Watch for the specific decay pattern: the block is missed once for a good reason, missed twice because the first miss made it feel optional, and by the third week it is deleted as clutter. None of those three moments felt like abandoning the plan.

- **Choosing too many focus areas.** Three is the cap in the template, and two is usually better. Five focus areas is not an ambitious plan. It is an unscheduled one. The weekly hour either splits five ways into 10-minute fragments that accomplish nothing or, more commonly, defaults to whichever area is most appealing that week, and the other four never get touched. Narrowing is uncomfortable because it means explicitly deciding not to fix things you know are broken. Do it anyway, and note the deferred areas at the bottom of the roadmap so the next assessment picks them up.

- **Consuming resources instead of practicing with them.** Reading a DFIR Report writeup is not the same activity as taking its artifact list and reproducing the analysis against a sample set of your own. Watching a Volatility walkthrough is not the same as running the triage on an unfamiliar image and writing a finding. Consumption feels productive and leaves nothing behind that can be checked six months later. The fix is to require each session to produce an artifact, such as a writeup, a parsed output with your annotations, a rule, or a cheatsheet entry. Prefer resources that make that natural. Use The DFIR Report with an active loop: read one, then match every artifact it cites back to the chapter in this book that covers it, and note the ones you could not have found yourself.

- **Skipping the sharing activity because it feels like an extra.** It is the highest-yield step in the whole exercise, and it is the one most often dropped. It is the only step that requires someone else's time, and therefore the only one with an obvious excuse. Teaching forces the reasoning chain to be complete in a way that private study never does. The audience's questions land precisely on the joints you had smoothed over. It also builds the habit Chapter 13 depends on, which is explaining technical findings to people who do not share your background. Book it externally, and pick the topic you are reinforcing rather than the one you are confident in, if you can stand it.

- **Treating the roadmap as a one-time artifact.** A plan written once and never revisited is a record of what you intended in one particular week. The value comes from the second assessment: the same table, rated fresh without looking at the old numbers, then compared. That comparison tells you whether the routine worked, and the causes it surfaces for a focus area that did not move are fixable once named. Put the six-month re-assessment in the calendar during Step 4, alongside the practice blocks, or it will not happen either.
