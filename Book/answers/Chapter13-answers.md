# Chapter 13 — Review Question and Exercise Answers

*Sample answers for the Review Questions and Practical Exercises at the end of [`Chapter13.md`](../Chapter13.md). These are reference answers, not the only correct answers — your wording will differ and that is fine. What matters is whether your reasoning covers the same ground. For the corresponding lab, see [`Labs/Lab13/task-answers.md`](../../Labs/Lab13/task-answers.md).*

This chapter's exercises are writing exercises, so the answers below show what a good output looks like next to a mediocre one rather than arriving at a value. Several exercises operate on material you produced earlier, such as a Lab 11 finding, a Lab 09 scenario, or your own past reports. The model answers for those describe the transformation rather than the specific text. Where a confidence level is named, it is one of the four defined in §13.2.2: Confirmed, Probable, Possible, Undetermined.

## Review Questions

**1. Why is a report best understood as an instrument for someone else's decision rather than as a record of the analyst's work?**

Because only one of the two forms produces an effect. The two are organized differently, contain different material, and are cut differently. A record of your work is chronological, organized around what you did, and includes everything because everything happened. An instrument for a decision is organized around what the reader has to decide. Anything that does not bear on that decision moves to an appendix or is deleted outright. Analysts default to the first form because their notes are already in it. Converting notes into a report by tidying the formatting is the most common reporting failure in incident response. The consequence is concrete: a perfect timeline sitting in a case folder while leadership makes an uninformed disclosure decision is a failed engagement, regardless of how good the forensics were. The test is not "does this document accurately describe my investigation?" but "will the person reading this make the right call because of it?"

**2. Choose three of the seven audiences in §13.1.1 and describe how the same finding would be written differently for each.**

Take the `update.exe` execution on WKSTN-221.

For the **incoming responder**, write the finding as a handoff. Give the SHA-256, the exact first-execution timestamp, the Prefetch filename and run count, the 4688 with the parent process and PID, the USN deletion record and reason code, and the fact that Amcache retained an entry. Then list the open threads in priority order, including the unparsed Amcache and Prefetch for FILESVR-03 and the path where they are staged. The responder needs what is confirmed, what was checked and ruled out, and what to pick up next.

For the **IR manager**, the same evidence becomes scope and resourcing. A confirmed malicious binary executed on one workstation two minutes after the phishing document opened. The attacker deleted it three minutes later, and the hash is in hand for hunting. Current scope is one workstation and one file server, and FILESVR-03 is unconfirmed and needs parsing today. If the hash lands there, scope grows to the finance share and a second analyst is needed. Artifact-level detail and parsing methodology are noise at this level.

For **executive leadership**, it becomes plain language and business consequence. A finance workstation was compromised through a malicious email attachment on the afternoon of 14 November. The attacker ran a program on it and attempted to reach a file server. The workstation is contained, the file server is being confirmed, and no customer data has been confirmed as accessed, with that last determination named as dependent on the file server review. The hash, the Prefetch name, and the event ID disappear entirely.

What does *not* disappear across the three is the uncertainty. It only changes form, from "not yet checked" to "unconfirmed and I need it parsed today" to "we expect to know by end of day."

**3. What is the difference between a fact, an inference, and an assumption in a written finding, and why do they fail differently when wrong?**

A **fact** is directly supported by a named artifact and reproducible by another analyst from the same evidence: *Prefetch file `UPDATE.EXE-89A4B22C.pf` records a first-run timestamp of 2024-11-14 14:21:03 UTC.* An **inference** is a conclusion reached from one or more facts by reasoning: *the binary executed on this host at 14:21:03 UTC.* That is very close to the fact but not identical. It depends on the reasoning that Prefetch generation implies execution, which is well-established but still a step. An **assumption** is treated as true without direct evidence because the investigation has to proceed: *the host clock was accurate to within a few seconds of true UTC.* They matter separately because they fail differently. A wrong fact means you misread an artifact — a local, containable error. A wrong inference means your reasoning was flawed but the underlying evidence still stands, so you can re-argue the finding from the same material. A wrong assumption moves everything downstream of it. If the clock was 11 minutes off, or the user was on leave and someone else was at the keyboard, every time-based conclusion in the report shifts at once. Findings that bury assumptions inside prose cannot be repaired when the assumption fails, because there is no single place to correct. You have to re-argue the whole report from scratch. Labeling them explicitly is what makes the report survivable.

**4. State the four levels of the confidence scale proposed in this chapter and give the test for each.**

**Confirmed** — supported by two or more independent artifact sources that agree, or by one authoritative source where no plausible alternative explanation exists. Test: *could I defend this in a deposition with only the evidence in the appendix?* **Probable** — supported by a single reliable source with no contradicting evidence. A benign explanation exists but would require an unlikely coincidence. Test: *would I be genuinely surprised to learn I was wrong?* **Possible** — the evidence is consistent with the statement but equally consistent with at least one plausible alternative. Test: *can I state the competing explanation? If yes, this is Possible, not Probable.* **Undetermined** — no evidence supports or refutes the statement, because the source that would have answered it was unavailable, cleared, not retained, or never generated. Test: *do I know which source would have answered this? Name it.* Three rules make the scale work rather than decorate the report. First, "independent" means the sources could not have inherited the same error. Prefetch and Amcache are written by different subsystems and are independent, while two exports of the same event log are not. Second, every finding carries exactly one level. If parts of a finding differ in confidence, it is more than one finding and should be split. Third, Undetermined is a finding rather than a blank. Publish the definitions inside the report and use only those four words. The undefined hedges — *likely, appears, may have, seems, presumably* — come out entirely, and so do the impression adjectives *sophisticated, advanced,* and *highly targeted*.

**5. Why must "Undetermined" appear as a finding in the report rather than being omitted?**

Because omitting it makes two false claims at once: it implies the question was not relevant, and it implies you looked and found nothing. An Undetermined row states something specific and useful: a question was asked, the source that would have answered it is identified, and that source was unavailable for a stated reason. "Undetermined — Security.evtx was cleared at 14:52 UTC (EID 1102); no forwarded copy exists in the SIEM for this host" tells the reader exactly how far the report's conclusions extend and hands them an actionable gap. Omitting the row tells them nothing and quietly invites them to assume broader coverage than you had. There is also a downstream reason. Every Undetermined finding points at a logging or retention gap, and that list is the work plan in §13.6.5. It is the one artifact from the incident the organization will still benefit from in three years. A gap tied to a specific finding gets funded. A gap presented as general best practice does not. Finally, there is a defensive reason, illustrated by the chapter's Example 3. An unqualified "no evidence of lateral movement was identified," written when the DC02 SIEM forwarder had been in a failed state for eight days, is the sentence that gets an incident reopened four months later by an auditor.

**6. What are the four components of a well-formed finding, and which one is most often dropped?**

**The claim** — one sentence, active voice, specific about host, account, time, and object. **The basis** — the artifacts, with enough identifying detail that another analyst can retrieve them: file names, record identifiers, event IDs, timestamps, byte counts, and an explicit note where two sources are independent. **The confidence** — one of the four defined levels, with a one-clause reason where the level is not self-evident. **What this does not establish** — the boundary of the claim. The fourth is the one that gets dropped, and it is the one that protects you. Every finding has a boundary, and if you do not draw it the reader will draw it somewhere more generous than the evidence supports. The chapter's Finding 4 illustrates this. Flow records and SRUM together Confirm a transfer between WKSTN-221 and 91.214.47.22: 184,221 bytes outbound and 2,415,891 bytes inbound. The boundary clause does most of the work. SRUM carries no destination, so the finding does not name `update.exe` as the process that made the connection. The large volume is inbound, which is the shape of a download, not a bulk upload. The session was TLS-encrypted and no packet capture covers the window. Any exfiltration claim therefore rests on the 184 KB outbound alone and is Possible, not Confirmed. Without that clause a reader sees a large byte count next to a suspicious address and walks away believing data was stolen, which the evidence does not support.

**7. Rank the following by evidence cost, least to most: EDR host isolation, hypervisor snapshot, graceful shutdown, perimeter C2 block, hard power-off. Justify the ordering.**

Least to most: hypervisor snapshot, perimeter C2 block, EDR host isolation, graceful shutdown, hard power-off. A hypervisor snapshot on a VM you control costs essentially nothing. It captures a complete memory image and disk state atomically while stopping the host. That is why it is the best-case containment, and why forgetting it in favor of the EDR console is such a common and expensive reflex. The perimeter C2 block severs the command channel while leaving the host entirely intact. Memory, processes, disk, and live state are all preserved, and even local lateral movement remains observable. Its cost is operational rather than evidentiary: the attacker may notice and rotate infrastructure, and the block does not stop on-host destruction. EDR isolation cuts all traffic except the EDR channel, so the host keeps running and memory, processes, and disk survive. Live network connections drop, though, and in-flight session state is lost. The attacker learns the host is contained, and the EDR channel now throttles collection. Graceful shutdown loses **all memory**, the most volatile and often the most valuable evidence. It also triggers shutdown scripts, service stop handlers, and any attacker cleanup wired to shutdown. Hard power-off loses all memory as well and adds file system inconsistency and lost writes. Its compensating property is that no shutdown handler fires. That is why it is the right choice when you suspect the handlers themselves are hostile, or when ransomware encryption is actively running.

Two entries in the full §13.4.1 table sit outside this list but are worth carrying with the ordering. VLAN quarantine costs about the same as EDR isolation and is the fallback when EDR containment is unavailable. Account disable looks low-impact because it does not touch the host, but it can terminate the exact processes whose memory you need. It belongs after capture rather than before.

**8. Under what circumstances should containment precede memory capture rather than follow it?**

When data is actively being destroyed: ransomware encryption in progress, a wiper running, an attacker deleting logs in real time. In those cases the evidence lost in the 90 seconds it takes to capture memory exceeds the evidence memory would have given you. The calculation is nearly always "cut the network now," and Chapter 12 covers it in the ransomware context. Outside of active destruction, the default sequence holds:

1. Document the decision and the clock: time, authorizer, option chosen, and why.
2. Capture memory. If the host is a VM, take a hypervisor snapshot instead, since it is faster and gives you memory and disk together.
3. Capture volatile network and process state — connections, process tree, logged-on sessions, open handles, scheduled tasks — in minutes, not hours.
4. Contain with the least destructive option that actually stops the harm.
5. Disable accounts and rotate credentials in dependency order.
6. Collect persistent artifacts at whatever pace the case requires, because disk is patient.

The point of the exception is that it has a stated trigger. "We contained first because we judged it was faster" is not the same as "encryption was running," and only the second is defensible in the case log.

**9. What are the three questions an eradication plan must answer, and what happens when any is answered "Undetermined"?**

**Have all persistence mechanisms been identified?** Not "have we found persistence" — have we found *all* of it. Run/RunOnce and Services are the ones everyone checks. The ones that survive cleanup are Scheduled Tasks, WMI event subscriptions, COM hijacks, IFEO debuggers, AppInit/AppCert DLLs, Winlogon shell and userinit keys, LSA and SSP providers, Office add-ins and templates, startup folders, BITS jobs, accessibility-tool replacements, and legitimate-but-attacker-controlled remote access tooling. A licensed RMM agent installed by the attacker is persistence. **Have all attacker-accessible credentials been rotated?** Every credential that existed in memory, in a file, in a browser store, in a script, or in a service configuration on any compromised host must be treated as known to the attacker, not just the compromised user's password. **Is the entry point closed?** If the intrusion began through an unpatched edge device, an account without MFA, or an exposed remote access service and that condition still exists, the intrusion has not been eradicated — it has been interrupted. When any of the three comes back Possible or Undetermined, that is a reinfection risk. Name it explicitly in the report rather than letting the eradication declaration paper over it. In practice, an Undetermined on any of the three should also push the rebuild-or-clean decision toward rebuild. Rebuilding from a trusted image is the one assessment that does not depend on the completeness of your investigation.

**10. Why does a defensible earliest-confirmed-compromise timestamp determine whether a backup is safe to restore from?**

Because restoring from backup is restoring from a point in time, and the only question that determines whether it works is whether that point precedes the attacker's arrival. That question is answerable only if the investigation produced a defensible earliest-known-compromise timestamp with a stated confidence level. Anchor it on the arrival of the attacker's first file, not on its execution. On WKSTN-221 the first attacker activity is at 2024-11-14 14:18:55 UTC, but the lure `Q4_Payroll_Review.xlsm` reached disk at 2024-11-12 09:32:14 UTC (MFT `$SI` and `$FN` creation). A 12 November backup taken after that moment can contain the document, and restoring it puts the lure back on disk. The restore point must predate the file's arrival. If dwell time is Undetermined because the logs that would have established it were cleared or aged out, there is no safe restore point at all. The honest statement to the business is that any restore point carries unquantified risk. The timestamp is necessary but not sufficient. You also select a restore point comfortably before it rather than immediately before it. You mount the backup content read-only in an isolated environment and scan it offline for the case's IOC set, especially its persistence artifacts. You verify that the backup infrastructure itself was not compromised, because backup servers are a primary target: compromising them defeats recovery. Then you restore into an isolated segment before reconnecting. Restoring a backup that contains the attacker's scheduled task simply reinstalls the intrusion. The timestamp makes that outcome a decision the business declines rather than one it walks into.

## Practical Exercises

**1. Take a finding from your Lab 11 case summary and rewrite it in the four-part form from §13.2.3, assigning one of the four confidence levels and writing an explicit "what this does not establish" clause.**

**What you should end up with:** One finding restructured into claim, basis, confidence, and boundary — with the boundary clause written last and written honestly.

**How to get there:** Pick the OneDrive channel finding from HR-WS17, because it is the strongest one available and therefore the one where the boundary clause is hardest to write. The claim: *Two of the three named pricing documents were uploaded from HR-WS17 to a personal OneDrive account (`hr.user.personal@outlook.com`) between 17:46 and 17:49 UTC on 2024-11-12.* The basis comes from four artifact families. The OneDrive `Accounts` registry key records the personal account alongside the corporate one. Two rows in the sync-engine database show `SyncState=UploadComplete`, enqueued at 17:46:42 and 17:48:41 and completed at 17:47:18 and 17:49:18, and their `Sha256ContentHash` values match the digests supplied by legal. The OneDrive client logs carry corresponding entries. SRUM attributes outbound bytes to `OneDrive.exe` in the same window. Different subsystems write these four families, so they are independent. Confidence: **Confirmed**. The boundary: this does not establish that the third document, `Margins_Q3.xlsx`, was uploaded. It has no row in the sync database, and the evidence for it is USB-only. It does not establish what happened to the uploaded copies after they reached the account, since no provider-side logs were obtained. And it does not establish authorization or intent.

**What good looks like:** The boundary clause names something a reader would otherwise have assumed, rather than restating a generic disclaimer. "This does not establish intent" is true of every forensic finding and adds nothing. "This does not establish that the third document was uploaded" corrects a specific inference the reader is very likely to make from the claim as written. Check the confidence level against its own test before you commit to it. For Confirmed, ask whether you could defend it in a deposition with only the appendix, and whether the sources you called independent really could not have inherited the same error. If you find yourself wanting to attach two confidence levels, split the finding. The chapter shows two ways to keep one level per finding. Finding 1 holds the execution chain at Confirmed and moves the user's action into a separate Probable Finding 1a. Finding 4 limits its Confirmed claim to a transfer between two endpoints. Its boundary clause then rates any exfiltration claim Possible, because that claim rests on the 184 KB outbound alone.

**2. Find three instances of banned confidence language (*likely, appears, may have,* and their relatives) in your own past writing or in a public incident report. Rewrite each with a defined confidence level, noting where you cannot because the underlying evidence does not support any level.**

**What you should end up with:** Three before-and-after pairs, plus an explicit note on any case where the rewrite was not possible.

**How to get there:** Search for the banned list — *likely, probably, seems, appears, may have, could have, believed to be, presumably, obviously, clearly, sophisticated, advanced, highly targeted*. Public incident reports and vendor threat reports are rich sources if your own archive is thin. For each hit, ask what evidence sits behind the hedge, then map that evidence onto the four-level scale. "The attacker likely exfiltrated data" becomes "Exfiltration of the staged dataset is **Probable** — supported by SRUM egress volume consistent with the staged set, with no per-file transfer record." If there is no egress evidence at all, it becomes "**Possible** — staging and transfer-tool execution were observed; no egress data covering the window was retained."

**What good looks like:** At least one of the three is a case where you *cannot* complete the rewrite, and you say so plainly. That is the instructive one. A hedge word sometimes covers a genuine gap in evidence. Forcing it onto the scale exposes that the sentence was never a finding but a guess with a softener attached. The correct rewrite is either Undetermined with the missing source named, or deletion. Pay separate attention to the last three banned words. *Sophisticated, advanced,* and *highly targeted* are not confidence claims at all. They describe your impression of the attacker, and they cannot be rewritten with a confidence level because they have no evidentiary content. They also have a documented habit of appearing verbatim in press coverage. Delete them.

**3. Write the same finding three ways — for an incoming responder, an IR manager, and a CFO — using the §13.1.2 example as the model. Keep the executive version under 80 words.**

**What you should end up with:** Three versions of one finding, visibly different in vocabulary and structure, all carrying the same uncertainty.

**How to get there:** Start with the responder version, because it is closest to your notes: hashes, artifact filenames, event IDs, PIDs, timestamps to the second, and an explicit "not yet checked" list with file paths. Then write the manager version by replacing artifact detail with scope, resource asks, and blockers — what the finding means for how many hosts, how many analysts, and how long. Then write the CFO version by removing every technical noun and answering three questions only: what happened, what it means for the business, and what is being done or decided.

**What good looks like:** The uncertainty survives all three translations and changes form rather than disappearing — "not yet checked, top open thread" becomes "unconfirmed, I need it parsed today" becomes "we expect to know by end of day." Smoothing uncertainty out of the executive version is the single most common failure on this exercise. It is also the version where uncertainty matters most, because someone makes a disclosure decision from it. Enforce the 80-word limit strictly. Going over is a signal that technical detail has leaked in. Finally, check the CFO version for the one thing it must not do: assert something the evidence does not support to sound decisive. Saying that no customer data has been confirmed as accessed at this time, and that the determination depends on the file server review, is both honest and useful. "No customer data was accessed" is neither.

**4. Draft a one-page executive brief for a Lab 09 or Lab 11 scenario using the §13.3.1 skeleton. Enforce the page limit strictly and note what you had to cut.**

**What you should end up with:** An actual one-page brief with all seven sections present — bottom line, what we know, what we do not know yet, business impact, actions taken, decisions needed, next update — plus a short list of what was cut to make it fit.

**How to get there:** Fill the skeleton in order and then cut to length from the bottom of each section rather than by deleting whole sections. Every section earns its place. The bottom line is the two or three sentences a reader who stops there must not be misinformed by. "What we do not know yet" carries an expected-by date on each bullet. "Decisions needed from leadership" carries a by-when and a because.

**What good looks like:** The page limit is real. Two pages is a different document, and readers skim it rather than read it, which defeats the purpose. The two sections most often missing are "what we do not know yet" and "decisions needed from you, by when." A brief without the second is an FYI, and nobody reads FYIs during a crisis. The cut list is the valuable part of this exercise: it should show you cutting *detail*, not *caveats*. If your cut list includes an uncertainty you removed for length, put it back and cut something else. The material you are tempted to cut for space is disproportionately the material that protects the reader from over-reading the brief. Finally, check the bottom line against the standalone test: read only those three sentences and ask whether someone acting on them alone would do something wrong.

**5. Run the adversarial reviewer test (§13.2.5) on a report you or a colleague has written. Produce a list of every sentence that fails, with the specific word or omission that fails it.**

**What you should end up with:** A line-by-line list of failing sentences, each annotated with the exact word or missing element that fails it, and each with a proposed rewrite.

**How to get there:** Read in the character of someone who wants the report to be wrong and has your evidence but not your goodwill: opposing counsel, a regulator's technical assessor, a peer reviewer in a claims dispute. Apply the five questions to each finding. Which word is doing work the evidence does not support? If this artifact could be produced by benign activity, did I say so and rule it out? Does any conclusion rest on a single source I described as if it were several? Is any assumption stated only implicitly? Would removing one finding collapse three others, and are those dependencies visible?

**What good looks like:** The annotation is specific to a word or a missing clause rather than a general verdict. "Overstated" is not a finding. "The word 'exfiltrated' asserts transfer, but the basis is staging plus tool execution with no egress record, so this is Possible, not Confirmed" is. Expect the pass to surface at least one instance of each of the three canonical weaknesses from §13.2: an inference wearing the costume of an observation, an undefined confidence word, and a finding with no stated boundary. Expect the dependency question to produce the most uncomfortable answer. Reports routinely rest three or four findings on one artifact interpretation without ever making that visible. The revised findings should come out shorter and more heavily caveated than the originals. That is the correct direction. An output where nothing got shorter usually means you reviewed in the character of a friendly reader.

**6. For a compromised host in one of the book's scenarios, write the containment case log entry described in §13.4.4 — time, option, authorizer, rationale, evidence cost accepted, reassessment trigger.**

**What you should end up with:** A single dated case log entry, three to five sentences, containing all five required elements plus the reassessment trigger, signed.

**How to get there:** Use the §13.4.4 entry as the pattern and adapt it to your scenario. A worked example for ACCT-FS01 from Chapter 12: *2024-11-12 09:40 UTC — EDR host isolation applied to ACCT-FS01. Authorized by [name] (IR Manager). Encryption ended at 03:38:41 UTC and the encryptor was deleted at 03:39:12, so no destruction is in progress and power-off is not warranted. Disk image acquired at 08:40 UTC under the preservation hold. Memory not captured: the host was still running when IR was engaged at 07:15, and no capture has been taken. Noted in the case file as a preservation failure. Isolation chosen over VLAN quarantine because the EDR agent is reporting and isolation keeps the collection channel open. Evidence cost accepted: live network connections dropped, and the attacker learns the host is contained. Isolation does not cost the memory, which stays capturable until the host reboots. Trigger for reassessment: if the EDR sweep for `.vaulted` files or `RECOVER-YOUR-FILES.txt` finds a second host, extend containment to that host immediately and re-evaluate whether estate-wide containment is required. — [analyst]*

**What good looks like:** The entry answers, in advance, every question a reviewer will ask about the decision. That is the whole point. Reconstructing the rationale nine months later is not possible, and an undocumented containment decision reads in hindsight as an accident. Two details separate a strong entry from a merely complete one. First, the evidence cost is stated as a specific loss, not as an acknowledgment that losses occur: "live C2 session state lost; ongoing attacker behavior no longer observable" rather than "some evidence loss accepted." Second, the reassessment trigger is a condition someone else could recognize, not a vague intention to keep an eye on things. Where a gap already exists before your action, as with the uncaptured ACCT-FS01 memory image, the entry says so and attributes it correctly. A case log that appears to absorb a pre-existing gap into your own decision is worse for you than one that names it.

**7. Build a persistence verification checklist for eradication covering every mechanism named in §13.5.1, with the artifact or command you would use to check each on a Windows host.**

**What you should end up with:** A table with one row per mechanism, each naming the registry key, file path, event ID, or command that checks it, and a column for the result on each host.

**How to get there:** Work through the §13.5.1 list rather than from memory, because the list is deliberately ordered from the mechanisms everyone checks to the ones that survive cleanup. Run and RunOnce under both `HKLM\SOFTWARE\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\` and the per-user `NTUSER.DAT` equivalent. Services via `HKLM\SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Services\` and System 7045. Scheduled Tasks via the task XML under `C:\Windows\System32\Tasks\`, Security 4698, and `Microsoft-Windows-TaskScheduler/Operational` 106/200/201. WMI event subscriptions via the `__EventFilter`, `__EventConsumer`, and `__FilterToConsumerBinding` classes in the `root\subscription` namespace — `Get-WmiObject -Namespace root\subscription`, or the `OBJECTS.DATA` repository file offline. COM hijacks via `HKCU\Software\Classes\CLSID\` entries shadowing HKLM. IFEO debuggers via `Image File Execution Options\<exe>\Debugger`. AppInit and AppCert DLLs via their `Windows` and `Session Manager` keys. Winlogon `Shell` and `Userinit`. LSA and SSP providers via `Lsa\Security Packages` and `Notification Packages`. Office add-ins and templates under the user profile. Startup folders for all users. BITS jobs via `bitsadmin /list /allusers /verbose` and `Microsoft-Windows-Bits-Client/Operational`. Accessibility-tool replacements — `sethc.exe`, `utilman.exe`, `osk.exe` — by hash comparison against known-good. Remote access tooling via Amcache, Prefetch, and installed-product keys, checked against what the organization actually licenses.

**What good looks like:** The checklist is per-host and produces a recorded result for every row on every in-scope host, including the hosts you cleaned rather than rebuilt. A blank cell is indistinguishable from a negative result three weeks later. The point the chapter makes is worth building into the structure: missed persistence is almost always in a location the responder did not check rather than one they checked and misread, and WMI event subscriptions and innocuously named scheduled tasks are the perennial winners. Add a column noting whether the check was performed live or offline against collected artifacts, since a live check on a host the attacker still controls can be lied to. Also add the row most checklists omit: legitimate RMM agents. A licensed ScreenConnect or AnyDesk install is persistence when the attacker put it there, and it will pass every malware-oriented check you run.

**8. Convert one behavioral finding from a lab case into a Sigma rule following the §13.6.4 pattern, then write the corresponding logging-gap entry for a source the case showed you were missing.**

**What you should end up with:** A syntactically valid Sigma rule with title, id, status, description, references, logsource, detection, falsepositives, and level — plus a logging-gap entry in the §13.6.5 form.

**How to get there:** Pick a behavior, not an indicator. The Outlook-parent-PowerShell relationship in the chapter's example is the model: it outlives the campaign, whereas the hash and the C2 IP are dead the moment the attacker rotates them. From the book's own scenarios, candidates include `rundll32.exe` invoking `comsvcs.dll MiniDump` against LSASS, `vssadmin delete shadows /all /quiet`, a process writing a `RECOVER-YOUR-FILES.txt` into multiple directories in rapid succession, or `rclone` command-line arguments (`--transfers`, `--multi-thread-streams`) regardless of the binary name. Write the detection block against the field names your platform actually populates, reference the internal case number, and put a real observation in `falsepositives` rather than "none." The chapter's example earns its low false-positive claim by citing 30 days of telemetry across 412 hosts. Then write the gap entry: name the source, state which findings it left Undetermined, propose a specific change, name an owner, and state the cost.

**What good looks like:** The rule generalizes the behavior one step beyond the exact case without generalizing so far that it fires on everything. It matches on the parent-child relationship rather than the specific PID, and on the rclone argument pattern rather than on `rclone.exe` by name. Tag it with the case reference. Three cases later, a detection engineer asking "why does this rule exist?" needs to find the incident that produced it. Rules whose originating threat is long dead should be retired deliberately rather than accumulating. The gap entry is where the real value sits: *Gap L-3: Security event log on workstations is capped at 20 MB and rolled over in approximately 36 hours on WKSTN-221. Findings 7 and 9 are Undetermined as a direct result. Change: increase to 512 MB and enable Windows Event Forwarding for the 4624/4625/4648/4688/1102 subset. Owner: Platform Engineering. Cost: configuration change plus WEF collector capacity.* A gap tied to a named finding gets funded. The same gap written as a general recommendation does not. The incident report is the moment to make that argument, because the window in which an organization will act on logging recommendations closes fast.

## Common mistakes on this chapter

- **Writing the report as a record of your work rather than an instrument for someone else's decision.** This is the root failure, and most of the others are symptoms of it. The tell is structural: a document organized chronologically around what the analyst did, with everything included because everything happened, and with the conclusion arriving on page nine. It happens because the analyst's notes are already in that shape, and cleaning up the formatting feels like writing a report. It is not. The correct move is to identify the decision the reader has to make, lead with what bears on it, and push everything else into an appendix or delete it. A technically flawless investigation that produces a document nobody reads has failed. The failure is invisible to the analyst, because the forensics were good.

- **Confidence inflation.** Every finding drifts one level up under pressure: Possible becomes Probable in the second draft, and Probable becomes Confirmed in the executive summary. The mechanism is almost never dishonesty. The analyst knows what happened, and the qualifiers start to feel pedantic. Watch for two specific forms. The first is calling two sources independent when they are not. Two exports of the same event log, or an EDR alert and the log line it was generated from, are one source wearing two hats, and a Confirmed built on them is really a Probable. The second is a finding that carries one confidence label while containing statements at two different levels. The rule in §13.2.2 handles this directly: if parts of a finding differ in confidence, it is more than one finding. Splitting it is not pedantry. It is the only way the label means anything. Run the deposition test on every Confirmed before release.

- **Treating "Undetermined" as a failure rather than a finding.** New analysts omit Undetermined rows out of embarrassment, as though the blank space is less damaging than an admission. It is far more damaging. The omission implies you looked and found nothing, which is a stronger claim than the evidence supports and one a reviewer will eventually test. An Undetermined row with the named missing source is genuinely informative. It bounds the report's conclusions, hands counsel something they can act on, and becomes a funded logging change in §13.6.5. The chapter's Example 3 is the case study. "No evidence of lateral movement was identified," written while the DC02 SIEM forwarder had been in a failed state for eight days across 40% of authentication traffic, is the sentence that gets an incident reopened four months later by an auditor. The Undetermined version of that finding is longer, less satisfying, and correct.

- **Containment choices that destroy the evidence needed to scope the incident.** Every containment action costs evidence. The discipline is choosing which loss and documenting the choice, not pretending there was no loss. Three specific errors recur. The first is reaching for the EDR console on a virtual host, when a hypervisor suspend and snapshot would have preserved memory and disk atomically at essentially zero cost. It is the most common and the most expensive error, and it happens because EDR isolation is the reflex. The second is disabling accounts before capture, on the reasoning that it does not touch the host, when killing sessions terminates the exact processes whose memory you needed. The third is a graceful shutdown on a host where you suspect attacker cleanup is wired to shutdown handlers, which is precisely the case where hard power-off is correct. Underneath all three is the same failure: containing before deciding what evidence the scoping questions will require.

- **Piecemeal containment that warns the attacker between steps.** Containment is visible. A capable attacker who watches hosts go dark one at a time will accelerate exfiltration, deploy ransomware early, burn current access to protect persistence you have not found, or go quiet for weeks. Containing broadly and at once, after scope is understood, gives the attacker one signal instead of a sequence of warnings with adaptation time between each. That creates a real tension with the pressure to contain the first host immediately, and preference cannot resolve it. The tension is the "monitor and collect" branch of the decision flow, and taking that branch requires both telemetry and explicit authority. What is not defensible is drifting into piecemeal containment by default because each individual decision looked urgent.

- **Declaring eradication complete without verifying it.** Eradication is verified, not declared, and the verification pass is a documented section in the report with dates and outcomes. Re-hunt the full IOC set across the estate. Re-check every persistence location on formerly-compromised hosts, including the ones you cleaned. Confirm the entry point is closed with a positive test rather than an assertion that it was addressed: the appliance version was checked, MFA enrollment was confirmed, the firewall rule was read out of the running config. Confirm credential rotation actually completed for every item, because partial rotation is common and usually surfaces during the next incident. Monitor with elevated sensitivity for a defined period, with the case's own detection content deployed and someone assigned to triage its hits. The four-day reinfection has five common causes, and only one of them, missed persistence, is a forensics failure in the narrow sense. The other four are failures of scope, sequencing, or coordination. That is why the analyst who did the investigation needs to be in the eradication planning conversation rather than handing over a report and leaving.

- **Defaulting to clean rather than rebuild.** The default should be rebuild, with cleaning as the exception that requires justification. Cleaning is an assessment made against the attacker techniques you happen to know about. Rebuilding from a trusted image is an assessment made against nothing at all, so it does not depend on the completeness of your investigation. When scope confidence is Probable rather than Confirmed, rebuilding is how you stop your uncertainty from becoming the organization's risk. One category is not a judgment call: domain controllers, certificate authorities, and backup infrastructure are rebuild-mandatory. A DC compromised at SYSTEM cannot be trusted after cleaning under any circumstance. A compromised CA means every certificate it issued is suspect — a forest-recovery conversation, not a host-cleanup one.

- **Rotating credentials out of dependency order, or stopping at passwords.** Rotating while the attacker still holds SYSTEM on a host that will see the new credentials accomplishes nothing but paperwork. The order matters. Break confirmed attacker access first, simultaneously with containment. Then rotate local administrator passwords across in-scope hosts, extending to every host that shares a password if LAPS is not deployed. Next come service accounts, sequenced with their application owners because this is where rotation causes outages. Then privileged domain accounts. Then `krbtgt` twice, with the replication interval between rotations, if there is any indication of domain-level compromise. Then the bulk user population, if credential dumping was confirmed somewhere many users had sessions. The most-forgotten category is last and is not a password at all: certificates, API keys, cloud service principals, OAuth refresh tokens, CI system PATs, SSH keys, and code-signing certificates. Refresh-token revocation in particular is a separate action from a password reset, and teams skip it routinely. The attacker then keeps their session after the rotation is reported complete.
