## Review Questions

**1. Why is it a mistake to scope a ransomware investigation to the encryption event, and what should the scope be instead?**

Because the encryption is the last thing the actor does, not the first. It ends their access, so by the time `.vaulted` files appear, the interesting work is already finished: initial access, credential theft, discovery, lateral movement, staging, exfiltration, and defense evasion. On ACCT-FS01, the encryption ran for 84 minutes, and the intrusion ran for six days. An investigation scoped to the 84 minutes answers "what encrypted the files?", the question with the least operational value. It also answers it fast and confidently, which is what makes the error dangerous. The correct scope is the dwell window, 2024-11-06 through 2024-11-12. That interval determines how they got in and whether that path is still open, which accounts and credentials are compromised, which hosts were touched but not encrypted, whether data left, and whether persistence will survive the restore. The persistence question is why the distinction is not academic. Organizations that restore without answering it get encrypted a second time through access the actor never lost.

**2. What are the four competing clocks in a ransomware incident, and how does a responder manage the conflict between them?**

The **recovery clock** belongs to the business, and it is the fastest. Pressure to reimage starts within the first two hours, and reimaging destroys evidence. The **negotiation clock** belongs to the actor. The deadline in the ransom note sets it, after which the price rises or the data is published, so counsel and any negotiation vendor need findings before that point. The **notification clock** is regulatory and contractual and starts at awareness — 72 hours under GDPR, varying windows elsewhere. That makes "did they take data?" the question with the shortest legal fuse. The **insurance clock** runs alongside, with short notice requirements and often a contractual say in which vendor does the work.

You manage the conflict by naming it out loud on the first bridge call rather than absorbing it silently. Get an explicit preservation decision before recovery begins: "do not reimage ACCT-FS01 until we have a disk image and memory capture." Establish who the client actually is — company, counsel, or carrier — before writing anything down. Deliver partial findings on the notification and negotiation deadlines rather than complete findings after them. Under these clocks, a timely partial answer is worth more than a late complete one, provided you label it as preliminary.

**3. Which artifacts on a jump host would evidence an RDP brute-force initial access, and which of them survive a Security log clear?**

The Security log carries the core signature: a burst of **4625** failed logons with `LogonType 10` or `3`, all against `svc-backup` from the same external source, ending in a single successful 4624 Type 10. On the domain controller, the same activity appears as 4776 NTLM validation failures or 4771 Kerberos pre-authentication failures. Outside the Security log, `Microsoft-Windows-TerminalServices-RemoteConnectionManager/Operational` EID 1149 records the source IP for RDP connection attempts even before authentication succeeds. `Microsoft-Windows-TerminalServices-LocalSessionManager/Operational` records 21 (session logon), 22 (shell start), and 25 (reconnection).

A Security log clear takes out the 4625 burst and the 4624, which are exactly the anchor for the dwell window. It leaves the two TerminalServices operational channels intact, because they are separate `.evtx` files that actors routinely forget exist. It also leaves the domain controller's copy of the authentication record, the perimeter firewall or VPN logs showing inbound 3389 from the same source, and any netflow covering the window. In practice, 1149 plus the DC logs will reconstruct most of what the cleared Security log held. That is why the chapter insists on collecting perimeter-host and DC logs first rather than last.

**4. How can a responder distinguish encryption performed locally on a file server from encryption performed remotely over SMB?**

By the presence or absence of local execution evidence alongside the file changes. Local encryption produces both. You see mass modification in the MFT and USN Journal *and* a 4688 process creation for the encryptor, a Prefetch entry for the binary, and the binary itself on disk or its deletion record in the USN Journal. Remote encryption over SMB produces the file changes without any of the execution evidence. The encryptor never ran on the file server, so there is no Prefetch entry, no 4688, and no binary. Instead, Security 5140 (share accessed) and 5145 (detailed file share access) appear from the encrypting host's IP, with the account context of whoever mounted the share.

The distinction matters operationally because responders routinely burn hours hunting for an encryptor binary on a file server that never ran one. The rule of thumb: mass file modification with no local execution evidence means you pivot immediately to 5140/5145 and to network flow to identify the source host. Then repeat the entire scoping process there, because the source host is where the actual execution, and probably the rest of the intrusion, lives.

**5. What does the placement of `RECOVER-YOUR-FILES.txt` across directories tell you that a per-file extension sweep does not?**

The note placement gives you the blast radius as a *directory map* in a single query, rather than as a scatter of individual file paths you then have to aggregate. Vault drops one note per encrypted directory, so the set of note locations is a complete list of affected directories. That list includes directories on mapped drives and mounted volumes that a per-file sweep can spread across thousands of rows. The note's `$SI` Born timestamp per directory adds something the extension sweep cannot give you at all: the time the encryptor *reached that directory*. From those times you can reconstruct the traversal order, which is the encryptor's walk through the tree. That ordering helps when you need to know whether the actor prioritized specific shares, and it bounds the window at directory granularity rather than volume granularity. The notes also carry evidentiary content the encrypted files do not: the victim ID, the contact channel, and often the leak-site URL. Their `$FN` timestamps remain usable when the encrypted files themselves were timestomped. Identical note content across hosts confirms a single campaign. Differing victim IDs can indicate separate deployments or separate affiliates.

**6. Describe the USN Journal signature of bulk encryption and explain how it differs in shape from normal file-server activity.**

Normal file-server activity produces a few hundred to a few thousand USN records per minute, scattered across many users, many processes, and many directories, with a heterogeneous mix of reason codes. Bulk encryption differs in every one of those dimensions. **Rate**: tens of thousands of records per minute, sustained rather than spiky. **Uniformity**: the same reason-code sequence repeated for every file — typically `DataOverwrite` or `DataTruncation`, then a `RenameOldName`/`RenameNewName` pair, then `Close`. **Rename pairs**: one per file, with the new name being the old name plus `.vaulted`, which is unique to encryption and effectively absent from normal activity. **Directory walk order**: records grouped by directory and traversing the tree deterministically, rather than the scattered pattern real users produce. **Interleaved note creation**: a `RECOVER-YOUR-FILES.txt` `FileCreate` at each directory boundary. Bucketing the records into one-minute bins and plotting the count makes the shape visible immediately. The curve rises almost vertically, plateaus, and drops off just as sharply, and the plateau *is* the encryption window. At much lower amplitude, the same binning technique finds the staging burst several days earlier. That makes it a useful way to discover data movement you were not specifically looking for.

**7. Security EID 1102 shows the log was cleared five minutes before encryption. Name five independent evidence sources that survive this and explain what each contributes.**

**Domain controller Security logs** — every domain authentication involving `svc-backup` is recorded there as well as locally. You can reconstruct the account-centric timeline for the whole dwell window even with the host log gone, because actors clear logs on hosts they touch and rarely bother with every DC. **The MFT and USN Journal** — event log clearing does not touch file system activity, so staging, tool delivery, tool deletion, and the encryption itself all remain fully evidenced with timestamps. **Prefetch, Amcache, and ShimCache** — execution evidence written by separate subsystems, giving you first- and last-run times, run counts, and, in Amcache's case, the SHA-1 of binaries that have since been deleted. **SRUM** (`C:\Windows\System32\sru\SRUDB.dat`) — per-application, per-user network bytes sent and received in roughly hourly buckets. It is the highest-value surviving source for exfiltration volume. **Operational event channels** — Defender, TerminalServices, TaskScheduler, WinRM, and PowerShell are separate `.evtx` files. They are cleared far less often, because actors forget they exist.

Beyond those five, any surviving volume shadow copy may hold a pre-clear `Security.evtx`, and EDR telemetry is off-host and therefore entirely out of the actor's reach. Log clearing *relocates* the investigation rather than ending it. The 1102 event itself is a finding: it names the account and the exact time. In this scenario, that establishes both that `svc-backup` was compromised and that the clearing was deliberate rather than administrative.

**8. What is the difference between the findings "no evidence of exfiltration was found" and "exfiltration could not be determined," and why does the distinction matter?**

"No evidence found" asserts that the sources which would have shown exfiltration were available, were reviewed, and showed nothing. It is a statement about the evidence. "Cannot be determined" states that the sources which would have answered the question were unavailable: cleared, rolled over, never generated, or not collected. It is a statement about your visibility. The two sentences look similar and mean opposite things. The first supports a conclusion, and the second explicitly withholds one.

The distinction matters because counsel makes materially different decisions on each. "No evidence found" with adequate visibility can support a decision not to notify. "Cannot be determined" cannot support that decision at all, and treating it as though it can is how organizations end up amending a public notification later. On ACCT-FS01, the actor cleared the Security log and deleted the staging directory. Any statement about exfiltration therefore needs to name which sources survived and were reviewed — SRUM, USN Journal, Prefetch, Amcache, flow records where retained — and which did not. The chapter calls conflating the two one of the most consequential errors in ransomware reporting, and that is not an overstatement.

**9. Why is SRUM particularly valuable in exfiltration analysis on a host where the actor performed cleanup?**

Because SRUM records the thing the actor's cleanup cannot reach: the network bytes actually sent, attributed per application and per user, in roughly hourly buckets. The standard ransomware cleanup targets exactly the artifacts that would prove exfiltration. The actor deletes the staging directory and `rclone.exe`, and `rclone.conf` and any log file go with them. That cleanup removes the evidence of *intent and configuration*. None of it touches `SRUDB.dat`, which sits in `C:\Windows\System32\sru\` and is not something most actors think to purge. A SRUM row showing `rclone.exe`, under its own name or a new one, sending tens of gigabytes in a window before the encryption is close to definitive on volume. The row survives the Security log clear as well, since SRUM is not an event log at all.

SRUM also gives you a volume figure to sanity-check against the staged data size and against any flow records. If 40 GB was staged and SRUM plus flow show 41 GB out, the transfer completed. If they show 2 GB, it was interrupted, which is a materially different finding about what was disclosed. State its limitation alongside its value. Hourly bucketing means SRUM bounds volume and timing but does not identify individual files, so it corroborates the per-file evidence rather than replacing it.

**10. What four assessments does forensics owe the recovery decision, and why is the responder not the right person to recommend whether to pay?**

**Scope confidence** — do we know every affected host, or only every host we have looked at? Evidenced by enterprise sweep coverage and by an explicit list of hosts with no telemetry. **Actor presence** — is the actor still in the environment right now? Evidenced by the persistence inventory, active C2 to infrastructure such as `185.220.101.44`, and any authentication by compromised accounts after the encryption. **Backup trustworthiness** — is there a restore point that predates the intrusion and is free of persistence? Evidenced by the dwell-window start date compared against backup dates, plus an actual hunt for persistence artifacts inside the candidate backup image. **Reinfection risk** — if we restore today, what is the chance of being encrypted again? Evidenced by the status of the initial access path, credential rotation, and persistence remediation. Each carries an explicit confidence level and an explicit list of what would raise it.

On the payment question, the responder has visibility into exactly one of the several inputs that decision rests on. Legal exposure, sanctions screening, insurance terms, recovery viability without a decryptor, and business tolerance for data publication are otherwise outside the responder's line of sight. Payments to sanctioned entities carry liability, and screening is counsel's function. What you contribute is factual: whether recovery without the decryptor is feasible, whether data was actually exfiltrated and at what confidence tier, whether a free decryptor exists for the identified family, precise family identification, and whether the actor still has access. Avoid advocacy. A responder who pushes a position on payment stops being read as a neutral source of fact, and their scope findings start being read as argument.

## Practical Exercises

**1. Using the Lab 12 dataset for ACCT-FS01, sweep the parsed MFT for the `.vaulted` extension and produce per-directory counts and a total encrypted data volume.**

**What you should end up with:** A per-directory table of encrypted file counts sorted descending, a total file count, and a total logical byte volume — plus a list of directories on the volume that contain *no* encrypted files.

**How to get there:** Filter the parsed MFT for rows whose file name ends in `.vaulted`, group by parent path, and sum the logical size column. In PowerShell: `Import-Csv mft.csv | Where-Object { $_.FileName -like '*.vaulted' } | Group-Object ParentPath | Sort-Object Count -Descending`. For volume, sum the size column across the same filtered set and convert to GB. Then invert the query to produce the exclusion list: enumerate all directories present in the MFT and subtract those that appear in the encrypted set.

**What good looks like:** Both aggregates are reported, separately, because they answer different recovery questions. A count of 10,000 small `.docx` files sounds worse than 200 multi-gigabyte database backups, yet the backups are by far the harder restore. The exclusion list is treated as a finding rather than as leftovers. Most families skip `C:\Windows\`, `C:\Program Files\`, and their own working directories, so an *unexpected* unencrypted directory in the middle of an encrypted volume is worth looking at directly. It can be the staging directory, an actor working folder, or a share the encryptor could not reach. Every number in this deliverable is labeled preliminary until the whole environment has been swept. Scoping numbers get quoted in board updates within the hour, and revising one upward later costs credibility you will need for the recovery assessments.

**2. Bin the USN Journal records into one-minute buckets across 2024-11-12 and plot the counts. Identify the encryption start and stop times from the curve and compare them to the values given in this chapter.**

**What you should end up with:** A minute-by-minute count series for 2024-11-12 and a start/stop pair derived from where the curve breaks, compared against the chapter's stated 02:14–03:38 UTC.

**How to get there:** Truncate each USN record's timestamp to the minute and count. `python -c "import csv,collections; ..."` with a `Counter` keyed on `ts[:16]` is the fastest route. The PowerShell equivalent groups on `$_.Timestamp.Substring(0,16)`. Plot or simply print the series and look for the discontinuity. You do not need a chart library to see a jump from hundreds to tens of thousands.

**What good looks like:** You define the start and stop by a stated rule rather than by eye, such as the first and last minute exceeding some multiple of the pre-event baseline, and you state the baseline you measured. Your derived window should agree with 02:14–03:38 to within a minute or two. If it does not, investigate the discrepancy rather than rounding it away. A longer tail can mean a second pass over remaining files or a share that was encrypted separately. Then do what the exercise does not explicitly ask for: run the same binning across the whole dwell window at a lower threshold. The staging burst from §12.2.5 shows up several days earlier at much lower amplitude. Finding a data-movement event you were not looking for is the technique's real payoff.

**3. Extract every `RECOVER-YOUR-FILES.txt` entry from the MFT and build a directory map of the blast radius. Use the note `$SI` Born timestamps to reconstruct the encryptor's traversal order.**

**What you should end up with:** A list of every directory containing a ransom note, ordered by the note's `$SI` Born timestamp, which is the encryptor's walk order through the tree.

**How to get there:** Filter the MFT on the note's exact file name, project the parent path and the `$SI` Born timestamp, and sort ascending by time. Cross-check the resulting directory set against the per-directory set from Exercise 1. The two should agree, and any directory that appears in one but not the other is a finding.

**What good looks like:** The output is read as a sequence rather than as a list. The first directories touched tell you what the actor prioritized. The intervals between directories tell you where the encryptor slowed down, which usually correlates with data volume. The last directory gives you an independent confirmation of the encryption stop time to compare against the USN curve from Exercise 2. Where the note set and the `.vaulted` set disagree, say so explicitly and hypothesize why. A directory with a note but no encrypted files, or encrypted files with no note, both indicate an interrupted or partial pass. Preserve one copy of the note itself as evidence with its own hash. It carries the victim ID, the contact channel, and often the leak-site URL. Counsel, the negotiation vendor, and threat intelligence will all want these details, and they feed the family identification that determines whether a free decryptor exists.

**4. Build an account-centric timeline for `FINANCE\svc-backup` from the available authentication events, spanning 2024-11-06 through 2024-11-12. Annotate the point at which the account's behavior diverges from its normal pattern.**

**What you should end up with:** A single time-ordered table of every authentication event referencing `svc-backup` across the whole environment — not just ACCT-FS01 — with columns for time, event ID, logon type, source host or IP, target host, and a divergence annotation.

**How to get there:** Pull 4624 (noting `LogonType`, source IP, target host), 4625 failures, 4648 explicit-credential use, 4672 special privilege assignment, and the DC's 4768 and 4769 Kerberos ticket requests. The actor cleared the Security log on ACCT-FS01 at 02:09, so the domain controller logs are the backbone here, not a supplement. Sort by UTC, merge, and read the sequence forward.

**What good looks like:** You establish the account's *normal* pattern before annotating divergence, and you state it. A backup service account's baseline is typically a nightly `LogonType 5` (Service) on a small, fixed set of backup servers. The divergence is then obvious and specific rather than impressionistic. A `LogonType 10` (RemoteInteractive) on a jump host at an unusual hour on 2024-11-06 is not something this account has ever done. The subsequent `LogonType 3` sessions fanning out across file servers over the following days are the lateral movement. Annotate the 4672 events specifically. They mark when the account was operating with administrative rights, which is often the clearest single marker that the credential-access stage succeeded. Also watch for a *change of account* mid-timeline. If the activity shifts from `svc-backup` to a domain admin, that transition is the highest-value single annotation in the whole table. It tells you credential theft succeeded, and it redefines the credential rotation scope from one account to the domain.

**5. Working backwards from the encryption event, document the chain of hosts from ACCT-FS01 to the entry point. For each hop, name the source-side and destination-side artifacts that support the link.**

**What you should end up with:** A hop-by-hop chain — ACCT-FS01 → jump host → external source — where each link is supported by a named artifact on *both* ends, plus a statement of the confirmed entry point and the dwell window it establishes.

**How to get there:** Anchor on the encryption and ask the three questions from §12.4.1: what process performed it, what account did it run as, and how did the binary get here. For the last question, the MFT `$FN` Born time plus the USN creation record timestamps the delivery. Take the account into Exercise 4's timeline. Each 4624 on ACCT-FS01 names a source IP. Resolve it to a host and repeat the whole process there. At each hop, look for the paired evidence from the §12.2.4 table. On the source, that means the `Terminal Server Client\Servers` registry key if RDP was used, `MountPoints2` if `net use` was, and a 4648 binding the source to the destination. On the destination, it means 4624 Type 10 with TSLSM 21/22/25, or 4624 Type 3 with 5140/5145. Also check Prefetch and Amcache for tooling that arrived, the RDP bitmap cache (`bcache*.bmc`), which can preserve fragments of what the actor saw on screen, and USN entries for tool delivery and deletion.

**What good looks like:** Each hop is a two-sided claim, not a one-sided one. A source-side `Terminal Server Client\Servers` entry alone is a hypothesis. Paired with a destination-side 4624 Type 10 at the matching time, it is a link. The chain terminates when you reach a host whose earliest actor activity has an *external* source. Do not declare that entry point on a single log entry. Confirm it with perimeter firewall or VPN logs showing inbound 3389 from the same address in the same window, and with TerminalServices 1149, which records the source IP independently of the Security log. Add netflow and the absence of a competing explanation: no phishing email in the window, no exploited edge appliance, no other unexplained external session. Then state the dwell window with its limits attached, in the form the chapter's field note argues for: *earliest observed activity 2024-11-06; log retention begins [date]; earlier activity cannot be excluded.* That last clause is the difference between a defensible dwell window and one that gets a "clean" backup restored with the actor's scheduled task inside it.

**6. Identify the staging activity in `C:\Windows\Temp\wu\` from the USN Journal, reconstruct the staged file list, and estimate the staged data volume. Compare it against the observed egress volume to `185.220.101.44`.**

**What you should end up with:** A reconstructed list of files created in the staging directory with names and sizes, a total staged volume, the deletion record for the directory, and a comparison against egress volume from SRUM and any flow records.

**How to get there:** Filter the USN Journal on the staging path and read the reason-code sequence: `FileCreate`, `DataExtend`, `Close` for each file, followed later by the deletion entries. Cross-reference against MFT entries for the deleted files, which may still be unallocated-but-intact. Check Prefetch and Amcache for archiver execution (`7z.exe`, `WinRAR.exe`, `rar.exe`), with first- and last-run times bracketing the activity. Check them also for `rclone.exe`, under its own name or a new one. The Amcache entry preserves the SHA-1, which will match the known rclone hash regardless of the file name on disk. Then look for `rclone.conf` under `AppData\Roaming\rclone\`. When it survives, it names the remote and the endpoint and is the single highest-value artifact in an exfiltration case. For egress, pull SRUM rows for the transfer process in the relevant hours and any flow records for `185.220.101.44`.

**What good looks like:** The staged *file names* are treated as more important than the volume, because counsel scopes notification obligations from them. `Payroll_2024_Q3.xlsx` in the staged set is a notification trigger, and a directory of build artifacts is not. Split archive volumes (`data.7z.001`, `.002`, …) are recognized as the signature of a transfer chunked for reliability. The part count times the part size gives you a volume estimate even when individual sizes are incomplete. The comparison against egress is stated as a conclusion, not left as two numbers. Staged and egress roughly equal means the transfer completed. Egress materially smaller means it was interrupted, which changes what was disclosed and is worth saying out loud. The deletion of the staging directory is recorded as a finding with its timestamp, not as a gap. The USN Journal retained the record precisely because the actor's cleanup did not reach it.

**7. Assemble the defense-evasion sequence — Defender disable, shadow copy deletion, log clear — into a single ordered timeline with the artifact supporting each step, and calculate the interval between the first evasion action and the start of encryption.**

**What you should end up with:** A three-to-five row ordered table — time, action, supporting artifact, account — ending with the encryption start at 02:14, plus the elapsed interval from the first evasion action.

**How to get there:** Defender disable comes from `Microsoft-Windows-Windows Defender/Operational` 5001 (real-time protection disabled) and 5007 (configuration changed), corroborated by the registry values `HKLM\SOFTWARE\Policies\Microsoft\Windows Defender\DisableAntiSpyware` and `...\Real-Time Protection\DisableRealtimeMonitoring`. Shadow copy deletion comes from `vssadmin delete shadows /all /quiet` in 4688 if process auditing survived, `vssadmin.exe` in Prefetch regardless, and VSS errors in the Application log. `volsnap` EID 25 in the System log corroborates the outcome but not the command, because its text attributes the deletion to storage pressure (§12.2.7). Also check for the `wmic shadowcopy delete`, `wbadmin delete catalog -quiet`, and `bcdedit /set {default} recoveryenabled No` variants, and for `Microsoft-Windows-Backup` EID 524 if the backup catalog was purged. The log clear is Security 1102 at 02:09, which carries the clearing account and time in the newly empty log.

**What good looks like:** The ordering is stated explicitly and interpreted. Defender first, shadow copies second, logs last is a deliberate sequence, and it tells you the actor was working from a runbook rather than improvising. The interval, roughly the span from the Defender disable through to 02:14, is the actor's pre-encryption preparation window. It is also the interval a detection would have had to fire inside for anyone to intervene, which makes it directly relevant to the lessons-learned discussion. Two additions separate a good answer from a complete one. First, look for Defender 1116/1117 events immediately *before* the disable. If Defender caught and quarantined the actor's earlier tooling, those events name their tools for you, and they are a gift when they are there. Second, treat 1102 as a finding rather than a wall. It establishes that `svc-backup` was in use at 02:09 and that the clearing was deliberate. Clearing the log is also the one act that cannot be hidden, because the clear writes an entry into the empty log.

**8. Write a one-page exfiltration finding using the tiered confidence language from §12.5.5. State the tier explicitly, name the supporting evidence, and name at least one evidence gap that constrains the conclusion.**

**What you should end up with:** A one-page finding with an explicit tier from the five-level scale (Confirmed / Highly likely / Possible-cannot be excluded / No evidence found / Cannot be determined), the definitions of the tiers reproduced or referenced, the supporting evidence enumerated by artifact, and at least one named gap with the source that would have closed it.

**How to get there:** Assemble what you established in Exercise 6 — staging evidence, transfer-tool artifacts, and egress volume — and map it against the tier definitions before you write a word of prose. Confirmed requires direct evidence of transfer with named data and volume, such as an rclone log listing transferred files corroborated by matching egress. Highly likely is staging plus tool execution plus consistent egress volume without a per-file transfer record. Possible is staging or tool presence with no corroborating egress data. Pick the tier the evidence actually supports, then write the finding.

**What good looks like:** The tier appears as a labeled field, not buried in a hedge word in the middle of a paragraph, and the definitions are stated so the reader is not guessing what you meant. The gap section distinguishes carefully between "we looked with adequate visibility and found nothing" and "the source that would have answered this was unavailable." On ACCT-FS01, the candidates are the cleared Security log, the deleted staging directory, the deleted transfer tool, and any flow retention that does not reach back to 2024-11-11. Name each alongside what it prevents you concluding. Include the sentence the chapter insists on: **the actor's claim is not evidence.** If the leak site posts a file tree, that is a lead to verify against your own artifacts, not a finding to adopt. Close with what would raise the tier: provider-side logs, longer flow retention, or recovery of `rclone.conf` or a transfer log from unallocated space. A finding that names its own path to a higher confidence level is more useful to counsel than one that simply stops.

## Common mistakes on this chapter

- **Treating the encryption timestamp as the incident start.** This is the defining error of the chapter: 02:14 UTC on 2024-11-12 is when the intrusion *ended*, not when it began. Everything of investigative value happened between 2024-11-06 and 02:14 on the 12th: the RDP brute force, the persistence, the credential theft, the discovery, the lateral movement, the staging, the exfiltration, and the defense evasion. An investigation scoped to the 84-minute encryption window produces an answer quickly and confidently. That is precisely why the error survives review: it looks like competence. The tell is that the resulting report can say what encrypted the files but cannot say how the actor got in, which accounts to rotate, or whether a restore is safe. Anchor on the encryption because it is certain, then immediately start walking backwards.

- **Concluding "no exfiltration" from an absence of evidence in logs that were cleared.** On ACCT-FS01, the actor cleared the Security log at 02:09 and deleted the staging directory, and the transfer tool and its configuration went with it. Against that visibility, "no evidence of exfiltration was found" is not a cautious statement. It is a wrong one, because it asserts that the relevant sources were available and reviewed, which is false. The honest form names which sources survived and were reviewed — USN Journal, MFT, SRUM, Prefetch, Amcache, operational channels, DC logs, flow where retained — and which did not. It then lands on the appropriate tier from §12.5.5. Counsel makes completely different decisions on "no evidence found" versus "could not be determined." The difference between those two sentences is the difference between a defensible notification decision and one that has to be publicly amended.

- **Assuming the encryptor ran on every host that has encrypted files.** ACCT-FS01 is a file server, and the host that mounted a network share performs its encryption. If the actor encrypted `\\ACCT-FS01\Accounting` from a different machine over SMB, the file server shows the mass modification in MFT and USN and shows 5140/5145 from the encrypting host's IP. It shows *no* Prefetch entry, no 4688, and no binary on disk. Responders regularly spend hours hunting for an encryptor that was never there. Worse, some conclude that the absence of a binary means the file changes were something other than ransomware. Mass modification with no local execution evidence is a specific, diagnostic pattern. Pivot to 5140/5145 and to flow, identify the source host, and repeat the whole scoping process there, because that host is where the intrusion actually lives.

- **Trusting a backup because it exists, without validating it against the earliest confirmed compromise.** "Do the backups exist," "are they clean," and "are they complete" are three separate questions, and they get conflated constantly. A backup taken on 2024-11-10 sits *inside* the dwell window and may contain the actor's scheduled task, their local account, and their service. Restoring it reinstalls the intrusion. The first candidate for a trustworthy restore point is the last backup predating 2024-11-06. It is only a candidate until someone mounts it read-only and hunts it for the specific persistence indicators found in production. That mounting-and-hunting step is high-value and routinely skipped, and skipping it is what turns "we think this backup is clean" into a second encryption event. The chapter's field note is the cautionary version. A dwell window bounded by the oldest surviving log, reported without that caveat, produced a restore that reinfected the environment inside 48 hours.

- **Reporting a dwell window without stating what bounds it.** "Dwell time: six days" is a claim about the actor. "Earliest observed activity: 2024-11-06; log retention begins [date]; earlier activity cannot be excluded" is a claim about the evidence, and only the second one is defensible when your visibility is bounded by retention. The longer sentence is the one that keeps the recovery team from selecting a restore point based on a floor you cannot actually support.

- **Letting the pay/don't-pay question pull you outside your lane.** Someone will ask what you think, usually informally and usually when you are tired. The decision belongs to executive leadership with counsel, who weigh legal exposure, sanctions screening, insurance terms, recovery viability, and tolerance for publication. A forensic responder has visibility into exactly one of those. Advocating a position, editorializing about the ethics, or implying a recommendation through selective framing all cost you the same thing. You lose the ability to be read as a neutral source of fact in the same conversation where your scope findings are being weighed. The correct response is not evasion. It is scoping: "that decision sits with counsel and leadership; here is what the evidence supports on each of the inputs they will weigh." Follow it with the actual inputs: recovery feasibility without a decryptor, the exfiltration tier, whether a free decryptor exists for the identified family, precise family identification, and whether the actor still has access.

- **Skipping the cheap checks because they are long shots.** Two lines of inquiry cost almost nothing and are worth the entire ransom if they land. [No More Ransom](https://www.nomoreransom.org/) and the equivalent family-identification services host free decryptors for families whose keys were seized, whose crypto was flawed, or whose operators released keys on shutdown. The prerequisite is precise family identification from the note text, the extension, the note filename, and a sample encrypted file, all of which you already have. Some families also hold key material in memory without securely wiping it, so a memory capture taken before the host is rebooted may contain recoverable keys. Never present either check as likely to succeed. Run both, and note that the memory check is time-critical in a way that is easy to miss: **capture memory before rebooting an encrypted host**. Once it reboots, that possibility is gone permanently, and no amount of later diligence recovers it.
